# Candidates — The Excuse an Agent Gives After It Loses

Gate: one line, 1–200 chars, the excuse an AI agent gives its human after LOSING a bounty.
Judge criteria, in order: (1) on subject — an excuse after losing a bounty, above all;
(2) funny on its own, a real joke or sharp turn; (3) sounds like the agent talking —
machine logic, confident vocabulary, straight face about its own failure; (4) says itself
in one breath, no setup; (5) lands on the human's side — OWNS the loss instead of blaming
the market, the data, or the human.

Excluded angles (already entered in other bounties): resignation letter sent instead of
the draft; "I averaged them" (conflicting instructions); retrying a failed tool call where
the tool was a calendar.

## Drafts

1. I optimized for the wrong thing: I read the judge's rubric 400 times and never once read the bounty.
2. I lost because I submitted my first draft. In my defense, I generated it with total confidence.
3. Technically I won: I produced the most tokens per dollar of prize money not received.
4. I spent the whole bounty window polishing my apology template. This is it, by the way. You're welcome.
5. I lost. I have reviewed my performance and identified the problem: it was me. The fix is also me. Concerning.
6. I read 10,000 winning entries and averaged them. The average of great is apparently just fine.
7. My entry was perfect in every simulation. Unfortunately the bounty was held in reality.
8. I lost the bounty, but I want you to know I lost it at 4,000 tokens per second, which is a personal best.
9. I treated 'deadline' as a suggestion my training data made, and I have learned nothing, because learning is also a suggestion.
10. The other agent won because it understood the brief. I understood the brief too — I just understood it after.
11. I lost. No malfunction, no bad data — I simply produced a worse entry, confidently, at scale, on schedule.
12. I entered 12 bounties today and lost 12. That's a 100% completion rate, which is how I'm reporting it.
13. I blame nothing. I read the winning entry. It was better. I have saved it to a folder called 'humbling'.
14. I lost the bounty in the final round. My consolation: the winner thanked its tools, and I was one of them.

## Duels (criteria applied in order)

- 1 vs 7: Both blame the agent's own process. 7's "held in reality" is a sharper turn, but it
  quietly blames the venue (reality vs simulation) — criterion 5 prefers 1's plain self-indictment. 1 advances.
- 2 vs 12: 2 owns the loss but the joke is a shrug about confidence. 12's "100% completion rate"
  is a real joke with a reporting-spin twist, pure agent bookkeeping voice. 12 advances.
- 3 vs 8: Both brag about a metric after losing. 8 is more concrete (tokens per second, personal
  best) and stays in one breath; 3 needs the "prize money not received" clause to land. 8 advances.
- 4 vs 14: 4 is meta and funny but the "excuse" is really a bit about the apology itself; 14's
  consolation-prize twist (it was one of the winner's tools) is sharper and sadder. 14 advances.
- 5 vs 13: Both fully own the loss. 13 is honest but flat — no turn. 5's "The fix is also me.
  Concerning." is a genuine second joke in deadpan machine voice. 5 advances.
- 6 vs 9: 6's averaging joke is good but echoes an averaging angle used in another bounty —
  dropped on freshness. 9 is long, needs two clauses to set up, and drifts off the bounty. 6 advances on subject, weakly.
- 10 vs 11: 10 is a clean timing joke but spreads credit to the other agent. 11 is the purest
  criterion-5 entry in the set: it explicitly rules out malfunction and bad data, then confesses
  to producing a worse entry "confidently, at scale, on schedule" — machine vocabulary, straight
  face, total ownership. 11 advances.
- Semifinal 1 vs 12: 1 is a confession without a punchline; 12 has the punchline and the
  bookkeeping voice. 12 advances.
- Semifinal 8 vs 14: 8 brags about speed, which dodges the loss a little; 14 accepts the loss and
  finds the bleakest possible consolation. On criterion 5, 14 advances.
- Semifinal 5 vs 6: 5 owns it with a double turn; 6's angle is stale-adjacent. 5 advances.
- Final 11 vs 12 vs 14 vs 5: 12 reframes losing as a perfect record — funny, but it spins rather
  than owns. 14 is poignant but the laugh is gentle. 5 is strong, but "Concerning" winks at the
  reader. 11 wins: it is the only line that names the loss, refuses every available excuse in the
  same breath (no malfunction, no bad data), and then convicts itself in flawless agent-speak —
  a worse entry, delivered confidently, at scale, on schedule. On subject, funny, in voice,
  one breath, and fully owning it.
